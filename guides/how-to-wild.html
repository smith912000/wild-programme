How to do WILD (wake-initiated lucid dreaming)

Updated 16 October 2026 · 8 minute read · By WILD

Short answer: WILD means entering a lucid dream straight from being awake. You let your body fall asleep while a small part of your mind stays watching, then you step into the dream already knowing it is one. It is easiest after about five hours of sleep, when you wake briefly and go back to bed.

What is a wake-initiated lucid dream?

Most lucid dreams start inside a dream: something odd happens and you realise you are dreaming. A wake-initiated lucid dream starts the other way round. You never lose awareness. You watch the drift into sleep, pass through the images and sensations at the edge of sleep (hypnagogia), and arrive in the dream awake.

Lucid dreaming itself is common. A meta-analysis of 50 years of surveys found that 55% of people have had at least one lucid dream and 23% have one at least once a month (Saunders et al., 2016). WILD is the deliberate version: you choose the moment instead of waiting for it.

When is the best time to try WILD?

Try it on the way back to sleep in the early morning, not at bedtime. Dreaming sleep (REM) gets longer as the night goes on, so the body drops into dreams faster after a few hours of sleep. At bedtime most people fall into deep sleep and lose the thread.

The usual set-up is called wake back to bed: sleep for around five hours, wake up, stay up for a short while, then go back to bed with the intention to stay aware.

How to do WILD, step by step

  1. Set a gentle alarm. Go to bed at your normal time and set a quiet alarm for about five hours later.
  2. Wake up properly, but only a little. Get out of bed, use the bathroom, read your dream journal or a few lines about lucid dreaming. A few minutes is enough for many people. Keep the lights low.
  3. Lie down and go still. Lie in a comfortable position. Many people find lying on the back makes the body sensations stronger, which helps WILD but also makes sleep paralysis more likely (see the sleep paralysis guide).
  4. Relax the body on purpose. Move your attention slowly from feet to head, letting each part go heavy. Resist the urge to swallow, scratch or turn over.
  5. Give the mind a light anchor. Count breaths, or count and repeat a phrase: “one, I’m dreaming; two, I’m dreaming”. The anchor keeps you awake inside; it should be light enough that sleep can still come.
  6. Watch the threshold, do not grab it. Colours, faces, fragments of speech and scenes will appear. Watch them as if from the back of a cinema. If you try to hold an image, you wake up; if you forget you are watching, you fall asleep.
  7. Let the body sensations pass. Heaviness, tingling, buzzing, a sense of falling or floating are signs the body is going to sleep. Stay calm and keep watching.
  8. Step into the scene. When an image becomes a place you are in, rather than a picture you are seeing, move into it. Some people imagine rolling or floating out of the body.
  9. Steady the dream. Look at your hands, rub them together, or touch the ground. Calm, slow attention keeps the dream going.
  10. Write it down. When you wake, note what happened, including attempts that did not work. The journal is how you learn your own pattern.

What does WILD feel like?

Each person is different, but the sequence is usually: a relaxed body, loose thoughts, flashes of image and sound (hypnagogia), then strong body sensations, then a scene that takes you in. The body sensations can include vibration, pressure or a sense that you cannot move. That last one is sleep paralysis. It is harmless, and for WILD it means you are close.

Common mistakes

  • Trying at bedtime. Most attempts at the start of the night end in ordinary sleep.
  • Staying up too long. A long wake period can leave you too alert to drift off.
  • Watching too hard. Effort keeps the mind awake. Aim for interest, not concentration.
  • Panicking at the vibrations. They are the doorway, not a problem.
  • Trying every night. Waking on purpose breaks up sleep. Two or three nights a week is a kinder rhythm.

The figures come from the International Lucid Dream Induction Study, where 355 people completed the second week. An earlier systematic review of 35 studies found that no technique yet reliably and consistently produces lucid dreams, and that most studies were small (Stumbrys et al., 2012). Expect practice, not a switch.

Questions people ask

Is WILD hard for beginners?

It is often called the hardest method because it asks you to balance staying aware with falling asleep. Many beginners get there by combining it with wake back to bed and a short, calm wake period.

How long does it take to learn WILD?

There is no reliable figure. Some people have a WILD in the first week; others take months. Keeping a dream journal and practising two or three mornings a week works better than forcing it nightly.

Can WILD cause sleep paralysis?

WILD passes through the same doorway as sleep paralysis, so you may notice it. Sleep paralysis is harmless and passes within minutes. If episodes frighten you or happen often, read the sleep paralysis guide and speak to a GP.

What is the difference between WILD and MILD?

MILD sets an intention to notice you are dreaming later, then lets you fall asleep. WILD keeps you aware the whole way through, so you enter the dream already lucid.

This guide is for education. It is not medical advice. If you have a sleep disorder or a mental health condition, speak to a GP or a sleep specialist before changing how you sleep.
